🫓The Roti Standard

Fly from Perlis to Sabah and a plate of nasi lemak in Vol. 1 nearly doubled. This time we expected the same story. It didn't happen.

Roti canai barely moves. Cheapest state to dearest, it swings just 35% — three states (Melaka, Negeri Sembilan and Perlis) tie for cheapest at RM2.00, and Putrajaya alone sits at the top with RM2.70. Compare that to teh tarik (57% swing) and mee goreng (70% swing, Perak's RM7.35 to Sabah's RM12.50).

Roti canai is the one food in Malaysia that costs roughly the same wherever you are. So we made it the ruler, and measured everything else in roti.

📏Everything, priced in roti

🧱The RM2 wall

RM2.00 is the single most common roti canai price in the country — 33 of 204 roti canai listings land on it exactly. Only 21% of all roti canai we found sells for less than RM2, and just 37% is at or under it. Malaysia doesn't have a smoothly rising price curve for its most basic food. It has a floor, and almost everyone is standing right on it.

🎯Your shop matters more than your state

The gap between the cheapest and priciest state for roti canai is 35%. The gap between the cheapest and priciest shop inside Perlis alone is RM1.50 to RM5.00 — a 233% swing, nearly seven times wider than the whole country's state-to-state spread.

Moving states barely changes what you pay for roti canai. Walking to the next stall changes it far more. If there's a consumer-facing takeaway in Vol. 2, it's this one.

🔗Does an expensive state stay expensive?

Not really. A state that's dear for roti canai tells you almost nothing about whether it's dear for teh tarik (the two barely correlate). Teh tarik and mee goreng move together more closely — a state that charges more for your drink tends to charge more for your noodles too — but even that link is loose, not a rule. Terengganu has the country's cheapest teh tarik and its second most expensive mee goreng, in the same state, at the same time.

🏛️The official number

The Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) publishes a national average roti canai price every year. The 2025 figure, published April 2026: RM1.56.

Our national median: RM2.20. That's a raw premium of about 41% — but DOSM's number is a 2025 average against our August 2026 prices, so adjusted for a year of inflation the honest premium is closer to 37%. We're publishing that number, not the bigger one.

For teh tarik and mee goreng, no official government price exists at all. We checked the full DOSM catalogue. Neither dish has ever been published. For two of Vol. 2's three dishes, this is the only price series in the country.

DOSM measures counter prices at sampled premises. We measure listed delivery prices. The gap between our number and theirs is delivery markup, platform commission and menu inflation combined — we can't separate the three, and we're not pretending otherwise.

📋How this was counted

Same rules as Vol. 1. Every price was read off a public foodpanda.my listing, plain versions only — no roti telur or planta, no iced or flavoured teh tarik, no mee goreng ayam or seafood. Where a page showed two prices, we recorded the higher, pre-discount figure. Roti canai sold in sets of two or more was converted to a per-piece price, with the original listing kept in the data so you can check the maths yourself.

After collection we pulled a 46-row stratified sample — two random prices from every state, plus the six most extreme prices in the whole dataset — and had it independently re-checked line by line: price, item, piece count, and whether the restaurant is actually in the state we said it was. 46 of 46 passed.

Where we're weak, we're saying so: teh tarik leans on nasi kandar and kopitiam menus rather than dedicated roti stalls, because roti specialists very often sell no drinks at all. A few states cleared the 10-per-dish bar with no room to spare. Full detail is on the Vol. 1 methodology page, which still governs both volumes.
